# Phishing: four ways a person gets played

## intro {hold=1}
Can one click move our money or open our network? [#four] Phishing plays a person in four ways.

## page
First, a login page that is not ours. [#fake] An employee types a password into a fake sign-in. [#real] Then the real site loads, so nobody notices. [#key] Consider {passkeys|pass keys} or hardware keys, because they refuse the wrong site.

## code
Second, the second factor gets talked out of the door. [#push] At 22:40, the phone asks to approve a sign-in from Amsterdam. [#third] The third prompt gets approved. [#cap] Consider a phishing-resistant second factor, number matching, and a limit on prompts.

## request
Third, a request from the CEO. [#ceo] On Friday at 16:50, finance is told to pay 240,000 euros today. [#gone] The money is gone in hours, and no filter catches it. [#call] Consider a call-back on a known number, and four eyes on every bank change.

## channels
Fourth, beyond email. [#chan] Text messages, phone calls, QR codes and chat apps. [#out] They sit outside our mail filters, often on a private phone. [#report] Consider controls that work on any channel, and a report button people use.

## good {hold=1.2}
What good looks like. [#click] Someone will click, probably this month. [#arith] That is arithmetic, not a training failure. [#q] The question is whether one click can move our money or open our network. [#fixes] If so, the fix is a {passkey|pass key} and a call-back procedure, not another awareness poster.

## first {hold=1.2}
A suggested first step. [#ask] Ask the CFO and the CIO for four numbers. [#a] The report rate within one hour of a simulation, and the click rate as a trend. [#b] The share of staff on a phishing-resistant second factor. [#c] The share of payments and bank changes verified by call-back.

## outro {hold=2}
One click should not move money. [#keep] Use passkeys, and a call-back.
