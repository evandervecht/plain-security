# Knowing what to protect: four ways we lose track of data

## intro {hold=1}
Which data would hurt most if it leaked tomorrow, and who owns it? [#four] Four ways we lose track.

## crown
First, nobody can name the crown jewels. [#same] The canteen menu and the M and A data room get the same permissions. [#thin] So the budget is spread thin, and the crown jewels get the average. [#list] A short list of five datasets, each with an owner, changes every decision after it.

## sprawl
Second, forty copies of the customer file. [#copies] It sits in the {CRM|C R M}, in exports, in two hundred mailboxes, and in every backup. [#test] Test systems are often loaded with real data. [#one] Protecting the {CRM|C R M} protects one copy out of forty.

## sharing
Third, shared with anyone with the link. [#click] Sharing is one click, and nobody revisits it. [#phish] Years later, one phished account opens every folder it could already see. [#default] Consider internal by default, and links that expire.

## retention
Fourth, kept for fifteen years, needed for two. [#never] Deleting feels risky, so nothing is ever deleted. [#leak] When the breach comes, all of it leaks. [#gdpr] Under {GDPR|G D P R}, keeping data too long is itself a violation.

## good {hold=1.2}
What good looks like. [#class] Three or four labels, and a named owner for each crown jewel. [#share] Internal-only sharing by default. [#test] No production data in test. [#ret] A retention schedule that actually deletes. [#gdpr2] {GDPR|G D P R} Article 5 limits storage, and Article 30 asks for a record of what we hold.

## first {hold=1.2}
A suggested first step. [#ask] Ask for one page. [#five] The five datasets that would hurt most, who owns each, where each lives, [#del] and when it is deleted. [#none] If that page does not exist, that is the finding.

## outro {hold=2}
Four ways we lose track. [#keep] You cannot protect what you cannot name.
