# Passwords and identity: who can get in, and who can do anything

## intro {hold=1}
Who can get in, and who can do anything? [#badge] Four gaps hand an intruder a valid badge.

## reuse
First, their breach becomes our break-in. [#leak] A breached webshop leaks a list of passwords. [#same] The same password works on our login, and it looks like a colleague. [#sso] Consider single sign-on with passkeys.

## code
Second, the second factor that is not one. [#call] Someone calls and says, IT here, read me the code. [#read] The employee reads it out, and the second factor is passed. [#keys] Consider passkeys or hardware keys, no text message codes and no shared logins.

## leavers
Third, they left. Their account did not. [#six] Six months later, four people have left, and four accounts still work. [#own] Nobody owns them. [#hr] Consider a joiner, mover and leaver process driven by HR.

## admins
Fourth, too many people can do anything. [#ten] Two admins became ten, and the last review was never. [#jit] Consider {just-in-time|just in time} admin, and a quarterly access review.

## good {hold=1.2}
What good looks like. [#perim] Identity is the perimeter now, not the firewall. [#q1] Ask how many people can do anything in our systems. [#q2] And when someone last read that list, name by name. [#finding] A list longer than a page, or an answer of not sure, is the finding. [#gdpr] With personal data, {GDPR|G D P R} Article 33 requires notifying the {Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens|Dutch data protection authority} within 72 hours.

## first {hold=1.2}
A suggested first step. [#ask] Ask the CIO and HR for four numbers. [#a] Logins through single sign-on with a passkey. [#b] Accounts still active a day after the leaving date. [#c] Standing admin accounts, and rights confirmed in the last quarterly review.

## outro {hold=2}
Identity is the perimeter. [#keep] Close the doors, and read the list.
